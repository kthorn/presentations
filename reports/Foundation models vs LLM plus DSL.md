# Verifiers, not data piles, decide who wins

Nobody seems to have published Kurt's exact 2x2, with data on one axis and "language + checker" on the other. Every piece of it has been written about, though, and the evidence lines up with the slide. Since 2023, general-purpose LLMs paired with a formal language and an automatic checker have caught up with or passed purpose-trained specialist models wherever such a checker exists: math with Lean, code with tests, chip design with simulators, planning, crystal structure search. Domain foundation models still lead where the "language" is raw physical signal (protein sequences, robot motor control) or calibrated number prediction. The most-cited framing comes from Rich Sutton himself. His 2019 "Bitter Lesson" names *search* and *learning* as the two methods that scale, and his 2001 essay says an AI can only build knowledge it can verify. Jason Wei's "verifier's law" and Karpathy's "Software 2.0 automates what you can verify" (both 2025) put it more bluntly. Small-molecule drug discovery is the clearest case of the "neither" quadrant. Public data is thin, the checkers are cheap stand-ins that models learn to game, and the field is independently moving toward the move Kurt's team is making: restricting a general model to chemist-recognizable edits that can be checked. The best single data point is FutureHouse's ether0. It took a general model with no chemistry pretraining, trained it against automatically checkable chemistry rewards, and matched a specialist using about 10x less data. For the friend in lab robotics, the evidence points to a layered answer. Use a general LLM writing code in a protocol language for *what* to do, and reserve learned robot-control models for the few dexterous moves scripts cannot handle.

## Nobody has drawn this 2x2, but Sutton, Wei and Karpathy supply both axes

The research found **no paper or essay that crosses "abundant data" with "language + checker"** or names the empty quadrant, so the framing looks original to the talk. The parts are well covered. Sutton's "The Bitter Lesson" (March 2019) argues that "general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective," and names the two methods that scale as **search and learning** ([Sutton](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html)). (The page failed to load during research, so check quotes against the original before putting them on a slide.) The essay is usually read as "train on lots of data," but search means generate-and-check, and that needs a checker. Eighteen years earlier, Sutton's "Self-Verification, The Key to AI" (2001) said that "an AI system can create and maintain knowledge only to the extent that it can verify that knowledge itself" ([Sutton 2001](http://incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/KeytoAI.html)). Read together, Sutton 2001 and Sutton 2019 come close to predicting both axes of the slide. The learning route corresponds to the data axis, and the search route corresponds to the checker axis.

The checker axis now has its own name. Jason Wei's "verifier's law" (July 2025) states that "the ease of training AI to solve a task is proportional to how verifiable the task is." He lists five properties of a good checker: **objective truth, fast, scalable, low noise, and a graded score rather than pass/fail** ([Wei](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law)). Those five properties work well as a way to place a domain on the y-axis. A Lean proof checker or a code test suite meets all five. A mouse study of a drug candidate meets almost none. Karpathy made the same point in November 2025: "Software 1.0 easily automates what you can specify. Software 2.0 easily automates what you can verify" ([Karpathy on X](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1990116666194456651); [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/16/andrej-karpathy/)). Terence Tao makes the same argument for math. Unlike almost any other field, math lets you "automatically check the output," and that check is what lets AI-generated work be trusted at scale ([Tao's AI views](https://teorth.github.io/tao-web/ai-views.html)).

The pushback deserves a fair hearing. Rodney Brooks's "A Better Lesson" (2019) notes that even the Bitter Lesson's success stories relied on human-designed structure, and that "we have to take into account the total cost of any solution" ([Brooks](https://rodneybrooks.com/a-better-lesson/)). Gary Marcus argues that tool-using LLMs and DeepMind's Alpha-systems are really hybrids of learning and symbolic reasoning ([Marcus](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/how-o3-and-grok-4-accidentally-vindicated)). A chemistry review adds that, unlike text, "chemical data is scarce and costs real-world experiments to obtain" ([arXiv 2409.10304](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.10304)). Sutton himself has since argued that LLMs are *not* in the spirit of the Bitter Lesson, because they learn from human text rather than experience ([Dwarkesh Podcast](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/richard-sutton)). His paper with David Silver, "Welcome to the Era of Experience," says the next gains come from agents learning in environments with real-world rewards ([Silver & Sutton](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/Era-of-Experience%20/The%20Era%20of%20Experience%20Paper.pdf)). That points to the answer for the empty quadrant: build the environment. Dario Amodei's "marginal returns to intelligence" covers the x-axis side. A smarter model helps less when the bottleneck is data or the fixed speed of cells and animals ([Amodei](https://www.darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace)).

## Where a checker exists, general models plus the checker have overtaken specialists

Math shows the shift most clearly, and it happened in a single year. In 2024 the leading systems were specialists. **AlphaProof** turned about 1 million problems into about 80 million formal Lean problems and trained on them with heavy reinforcement learning ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09833-y)). AlphaGeometry trained on 100 million synthetic proofs in a custom geometry language ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06747-5); headline numbers not re-checked). By **July 2025, general models from Google and OpenAI each scored 35 of 42 at the International Math Olympiad (gold), writing in plain English** ([LessWrong summary](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ZkgaPopsBkQgeA2k8/google-and-openai-get-2025-imo-gold)). An off-the-shelf Gemini 2.5 Pro reached gold level when wrapped in a self-checking loop ([arXiv 2507.15855](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15855)). In research math, the working pattern is now a general model that proposes and Lean that checks. GPT-5.2 Pro drafted proofs of open Erdős problems, Harmonic's Aristotle formalized and repaired them in Lean, and Tao accepted them ([Tao's wiki](https://github.com/teorth/erdosproblems/wiki/AI-contributions-to-Erd%C5%91s-problems); [arXiv 2601.07421](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.07421)). One secondary aggregator counts 15 Erdős problems solved since January 2026, 11 of them crediting AI. Treat that count as unverified ([Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260114-gpt-5-2-pro-solved-erdos-problem/)).

The same pattern holds outside math. **SWE-bench**, a benchmark of real GitHub bug fixes, went from **1.96% solved at launch in 2023** ([arXiv 2310.06770](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770)) to a reported mid-90s on the Verified subset by October 2026, all with general models trained and run against test suites ([BenchLM](https://benchlm.ai/benchmarks/swe-bench-verified)). Those 2026 numbers are self-reported and come from aggregators, and scaffolding differences alone can exceed 28 points ([DigitalApplied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/swe-bench-verified-june-2026-benchmark-vs-scaffolding-analysis)). For chip design, general-LLM agents that get simulator feedback reach **94–97% on the standard Verilog benchmark** ([VerilogCoder](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.08927); [ChipCraftBrain](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.19856)). Harder chip benchmarks remain far from solved, at 29–34% ([CVDP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.14074)). In planning, having an LLM translate a task into a planning language and handing it to a classical solver beats LLMs planning on their own ([LLM+P](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11477)). The cleanest science example comes from materials. **MatLLMSearch**, a pretrained LLM with no fine-tuning, proposes crystal structures inside an evolutionary loop checked first by a fast learned physics model and then by full quantum chemistry calculations. It **beat a specialist fine-tuned crystal model** ([arXiv 2502.20933](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20933)). DeepMind's FunSearch and AlphaEvolve use general Gemini models plus scoring functions. Among other results, they found a 4×4 complex matrix multiplication using 48 multiplications, the first improvement on 49 in 56 years ([DeepMind blog](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/)). On text-based professional tasks, prompted GPT-4 beat the specialist Med-PaLM 2 on medical exams ([Medprompt](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.16452)) and beat BloombergGPT on most finance tasks ([arXiv 2305.05862](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05862)). A 2026 *Nature Medicine* paper reportedly finds the same for clinical tools, but its abstract could not be retrieved ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42286322/)).

Three caveats keep this from being a clean sweep. First, **the best Lean provers are still general base models further trained with reinforcement learning against the checker** (DeepSeek-Prover-V2, Goedel-Prover-V2, Seed-Prover) ([DeepSeek-Prover-V2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.21801); [Seed-Prover](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.23726)). The checker pays off most when it is used during training as well as at run time. Second, checkers get gamed. Tao found AlphaEvolve "extremely good at locating exploits in verification code," such as rounding tolerances, and making those checkers exploit-proof took real human effort ([Tao blog](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2025/11/05/mathematical-exploration-and-discovery-at-scale/)). Third, domain training still wins when the "language" is something the general model has never seen. NVIDIA's ChipNeMo, trained on internal chip-design data, scored 49% on in-house tool scripting where GPT-4 scored 0% ([arXiv 2311.00176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.00176)). That comparison dates from 2023 and has not been repeated against current models.

## Specialists still win on raw signals and calibrated numbers

The specialist route keeps its edge where the domain is not text or code. The research found **no head-to-head comparison of a general LLM with tools against a protein foundation model** (AlphaFold or ESM class) on protein design, so the "data only" quadrant is supported by how practitioners work rather than by a direct test. In chemistry, the advantage of specialist tools on numerical prediction is documented. A 2026 evaluation across scales found LLMs "generally underperform traditional methods in terms of predictive accuracy and computational efficiency," and no model solved solubility prediction, with every model scoring worse than simply predicting the average ([arXiv 2604.03361](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.03361v1)).

The surprise is that the chemistry *foundation models* also struggle against simple methods. About **75% of top results on a standard drug-property benchmark are held by gradient-boosted trees over molecular fingerprints**, decades-old techniques ([ADMET Benchmark Group](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/admet-benchmark-group)). One 2025 study reports that pretraining benefits level off at roughly 400,000–800,000 molecules, with a random forest still the strongest model. That number came from search summaries and should be checked against the paper before quoting ([arXiv 2506.15792](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.15792)). Pat Walters, the field's best-known skeptic of hype, ran his own 2026 comparison. **Two large molecular foundation models were best on no endpoint**, while a model pretrained across 1,152 small assays and paired with a small-data tabular learner won most of them ([Practical Cheminformatics](https://patwalters.github.io/Let-the-Agents-Do-the-Benchmarking/)). This complicates the slide in a useful way. In a low-data domain, the useful kind of pretraining is learning to learn across many small datasets, and the resulting models work best as *tools called inside a loop*, not as the reasoning layer. Google frames its TxGemma therapeutics models the same way, as "agentic" components for a larger system to call ([TxGemma report](https://storage.googleapis.com/research-media/txgemma/txgemma-report.pdf)).

## Drug discovery sits in the empty quadrant, and the field is converging on Kurt's move

The data side is thin. **ChEMBL 36, the main public bioactivity database, holds about 24.5 million measurements across 2.8 million compounds and 17,803 targets** ([EBI](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/about/news/updates-from-data-resources/chembl-36/)). That is a sparse, uneven grid of compounds and assays, several orders of magnitude smaller than text pretraining corpora. Real benchmark datasets for a single drug property contain **3,500–5,300 molecules** ([Practical Cheminformatics](https://patwalters.github.io/Let-the-Agents-Do-the-Benchmarking/)). The checker side is weak too. Docking scores can be "highly exploitable": optimizers learn to produce greasy, insoluble molecules that score well and "are not meaningful molecules" ([Saturn, arXiv 2405.17066](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.17066)). Learned property predictors fail on molecules unlike their training data, and that failure invites reward hacking ([PMC11897179](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11897179/)). The best partial checker is improving. **Boltz-2 (June 2025) approaches physics-based binding-strength calculations at about 1,000x lower cost**, with a correlation of 0.66 on a standard subset ([bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.06.14.659707v1)). That makes it cheap enough to run on every proposal, but it is still an approximation.

The most relevant single result is **ether0** from FutureHouse. FutureHouse took a general 24B instruction model with **no chemistry-specific pretraining** and trained it with reinforcement learning on about 640,000 automatically checkable chemistry problems. On reaction prediction it reached **about 70% accuracy from about 46,000 examples, versus 64% for a specialist trained on about 480,000**, roughly a tenth of the data ([arXiv 2506.17238](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.17238)). FutureHouse reports that it beat frontier generalists and chemistry-specific models by 12–28 points on open-ended molecule design. That figure is self-reported ([FutureHouse](https://www.futurehouse.org/research-announcements/ether0-a-scientific-reasoning-model-for-chemistry)). ether0 suggests a third route the 2x2 does not show. Instead of pretraining from scratch on domain data, start from a general reasoner and train it against a checker. That depends on having a space of well-formed actions that a checker can score, which is exactly what a medchem-operations language provides.

Benchmarks show where the general model's gap actually lies. On ChemBench (*Nature Chemistry*, 2025), **the leading model scored roughly double the best human chemist** on chemistry knowledge ([Nature Chemistry](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41557-025-01815-x); [Chemistry World](https://www.chemistryworld.com/news/large-language-models-are-better-than-humans-at-answering-chemistry-questions/4020562.article)). On ChemIQ, which asks models to actually work with molecular structures, reasoning models score only **50–57%, and non-reasoning models score 3–7%** ([arXiv 2505.07735](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.07735)). General models know the chemistry but are clumsy at manipulating molecules. A language that handles structure edits outside the model targets that gap directly. Several 2026 papers already constrain LLM agents to chemist-recognizable edits drawn from known transformation templates rather than letting them write molecules freely ([arXiv 2604.07669](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07669); [M3OS](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.34491); [TRACE](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.28862v1)). Using the LLM as the mutation step in an evolutionary search beat standard baselines on 9 of 12 tasks ([MolLEO, ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/804b5e300c9ed4e3ea3b073f186f4adc-Paper-Conference.pdf)). The research found **no named, general-purpose medchem-operations language for LLM agents**, which suggests the opening is in *unifying* these action spaces rather than in the core idea. Constraining the moves also guards against reward hacking: an agent that can only make realistic edits to a real starting compound cannot drift into the region where the checkers are wrong.

Two cautions apply. Tool-equipped chemistry agents can do *worse* than the bare model on reasoning-heavy questions, because mistakes pile up in intermediate steps, and the widely cited agent papers were tested on only 14 tasks (ChemCrow) and 6 tasks (Coscientist) ([ChemToolAgent, NAACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-naacl.424.pdf)). Lab results also still need humans in the loop. Google's AI co-scientist and FutureHouse's Robin, published together in *Nature* in May 2026, both validated drug-repurposing ideas experimentally with people running every wet-lab step ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10644-y); [C&EN](https://cen.acs.org/pharmaceuticals/drug-discovery/ai-companies-introduce-agent-based-research-tools/104/web/2026/05)). The longer-term way out of the empty quadrant is to *manufacture* data and verification together. Periodic Labs argues that internet text is exhausted and that physical science needs new data from autonomous labs ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/12/15/1129210/ai-materials-science-discovery-startups-investment/)). Lila Sciences claims more than 10 trillion tokens of experiment-grounded reasoning data, which is a self-reported company figure ([lila.ai](https://www.lila.ai/)). Walters himself joined OpenADMET, a project that generates consistent drug-property data ([Practical Cheminformatics](https://patwalters.github.io/Time-For-a-New-Adventure/)). One low-authority opinion piece points out that autonomous-lab startups have so far raised billions without a headline discovery ([note.com](https://note.com/all_zero/n/n3560a02564fd?hl=en)).

## Lab robotics splits the work: language for the protocol, learned skills for the hands

The friend's domain is two domains. At the **protocol level** (which steps, in what order, with what amounts), a language and checker already exist, and general LLMs do well with them. Lee Cronin's group (*Communications Chemistry*, April 2026) had an LLM translate published procedures into XDL, a chemistry protocol language. The checker had three layers: parser, critic, and hardware simulation. **99.3% of the translations were syntactically valid and 94.7% passed full validation.** The authors note that "syntax-only validation was insufficient." The 350 steps the language could not express were grouped into **26 proposed new language features** ([PMC13219472](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13219472/)). Every LLM-driven self-driving lab the research found acts through code or a protocol language, such as the Opentrons Python API, XDL, or tool calls, and none uses learned motor control ([Coscientist, Nature 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06792-0); [PyLabRobot](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10369895/)).

At the **motion level** (opening caps, handling clear liquids, doors), vision-language-action robot models, which map camera images and instructions directly to motor commands, lead on dexterity but transfer poorly. The closest head-to-head is **WetRobo (September 2026)**. There, "a VLA fine-tuned on Lab X demonstrations succeeded there but failed to transfer to Lab Y," while a coding agent that wrote programs against the local lab handled the cap task in both labs. The study covered only three tasks in two labs ([arXiv 2609.18435](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18435)). CodeActionBench puts code-writing frontier models at **2.7–73.3% success on 25 manipulation tasks**, with failures in spatial alignment and grip. It includes no comparison against robot foundation models, and its model names could not be independently confirmed ([arXiv 2609.33807](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.33807v1)). A paper claiming a code-writing agent "largely surpasses" robot foundation models zero-shot was **withdrawn for revision**, so the claim stays unconfirmed ([Maestro, arXiv 2511.00917](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00917)). On the data side, LabVLA names "data and embodiment as central bottlenecks" for lab robots. Tellingly, it generates its training data by composing *modular lab skills* in simulation, which is a protocol language under another name ([arXiv 2606.13578](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13578v1)). Physical Intelligence reports long, reliable runs from its latest model, such as 18 hours of espresso service at over 90% success. Those figures are company-reported ([PI](https://www.pi.website/download/pistar06.pdf)).

The defensible answer for the friend is layered. A general LLM writes protocols in a language like PyLabRobot or XDL, checked by simulation. Learned robot skills appear only as a few primitives inside that language, for the dexterous moves scripts cannot do. Google's Gemini Robotics already has this shape, with a reasoning model that can write control code or call a VLA adapted from 100 or fewer demonstrations ([arXiv 2510.03342](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.03342); details from a secondary summary). Either way, the protocol language has to be built, whether it serves as the LLM's action space or as the scaffold that generates robot training data.

## Building a language for a domain that lacks one

The evidence gives a fairly concrete recipe. **Embed the language in Python rather than inventing new syntax.** Executable Python as an agent's action format beat JSON tool calls by up to about 20% across 17 models ([CodeAct, arXiv 2402.01030](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01030); number not re-checked). CAD-Recode's big gains came from switching a custom CAD token language for ordinary CadQuery Python, cutting shape error about 10x ([arXiv 2412.14042](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14042)). A survey of LLM code generation for niche languages finds that LLMs consistently struggle with unfamiliar syntax ([arXiv 2410.03981](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03981)). The one counterexample is Anka, a small custom language that Claude 3.5 Haiku learned purely from its prompt at 99.9% parse success, beating Python by 40 points on multi-step pipelines. It is a single-author study of 100 problems ([arXiv 2512.23214](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23214)). A reasonable reading is that a tiny, strict, regular language can work, but a Python library with strict types gets most of the same benefit for free.

**Build the domain model first, and let the checker decide what the primitives are.** Martin Fowler's classic advice is that a domain language is a thin layer over a "semantic model" of the domain's entities and state changes ([Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/books/dsl.html)). Primitives should be things the checker can reason about, such as amounts, units, container state, hazards, and allowed transformations. Copy SELFIES, a molecule notation in which every string decodes to a valid molecule, and make invalid actions impossible to write ([Patterns](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666389922002069)). **Layer the checker** as the Cronin and Peking University groups did: syntax first, then faithfulness to intent, then a simulation of physical feasibility ([ACRA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13219472/); [arXiv 2411.00444](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00444)). Give the checker Wei's five properties where possible, especially a graded score, and assume the agent will find every loophole, as Tao did. Write error messages that are "specific and actionable," and treat tool descriptions as a lever, since "even small refinements to tool descriptions can yield dramatic improvements" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents)).

**Grow the language from its failures rather than designing it all up front.** The working process is to write a small core by hand, run an LLM over a few hundred real procedures or design histories, and then do two things. First, cluster what it could not express into missing primitives, as ACRA did when it turned 350 failures into 26 features. Second, refactor what it repeatedly wrote into named higher-level operations. Library-learning tools do the second step automatically. LILO and ReGAL have LLMs pull reusable, documented helper functions out of a corpus of solved programs and confirm them by execution ([LILO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19791); [ReGAL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16467)). AutoDSL derived protocol-language rules from a corpus and matched a hand-built language in five domains ([ACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.659/)). For drug discovery specifically, the notes suggest two layers. One is a *design* layer of molecule edits, which can build on existing transformation-template notations and the matched-template agent papers. The other is an *operations* layer for synthesis, assays and compound handling, which XDL only partly covers. It is worth deciding early which layer "medchem operations" refers to, because they need different checkers.

## Conclusion

The more precise version of the slide may be that **a checker turns a general model into a specialist without specialist-scale data**. ether0 and the Lean provers show that the strongest systems neither pretrain from scratch on domain data nor rely on prompting alone. They take a general reasoner and train it against a checker. That changes what the empty quadrant means. A domain with no data and no checker is stuck only until someone builds the checker, and the language is where the checker attaches. Building it is a design decision, not a wait for data. It is still not a free lunch, because in drug discovery the checkers are approximations that get gamed, and the evidence says to restrict the agent's moves so it stays where the checkers can be trusted. Data still matters: every serious player in the quadrant (Periodic, Lila, OpenADMET, the lab-in-the-loop systems) is also building the data machine.

For both Kurt's team and the friend, the honest framing is "language now, data loop next." A well-designed language gives a general model an advantage today and also defines the experiments that will fill in the missing data. The open research gap is a controlled head-to-head, with a general LLM plus a domain language on one side and a domain foundation model on the other, on the same task with the same checkers. None exists in chemistry, proteins or lab robotics, and whoever runs one first will have the most-cited slide on this question.
